# Bookkeeping fundamentals for a shop

Bookkeeping is the daily habit of recording every rupee that comes in and goes out. Good books tell you your real profit, who owes you money, whom you owe, and how much stock you hold. Without them you are guessing, and guessing is how small shops slowly lose money.

In a simple shop ledger, a credit entry means you gave goods or money on udhaar, so the customer now owes you more. A debit entry means the customer paid you back or you received money, reducing what they owe. A party's balance is the total of credits minus debits — a positive balance means they still owe you.

The day book (roznamcha) records transactions in date order as they happen. From it, amounts are grouped party by party into the ledger, so you can see each customer's or supplier's running balance at a glance. Recording in the day book the same day keeps it accurate.

Receivables are the amounts customers owe you (your udhaar given out); payables are the amounts you owe suppliers. Healthy cash flow means collecting receivables on time while paying payables on agreed terms. Watching both sides stops a profitable shop from running out of cash.

The difference between cash basis and accrual basis is timing. Cash basis records money only when it actually moves; accrual records a sale or expense when it happens even if payment comes later. Most small shops use cash basis for simplicity, but tracking udhaar separately gives you the accrual picture too.

Profit is sales minus the cost of goods sold minus expenses. Gross profit is sales minus only the cost of goods; net profit subtracts rent, salaries, electricity and other running costs. Knowing both tells you whether your prices are high enough and whether your overheads are under control.

A balance sheet is a snapshot of what the business owns (assets like cash, stock, equipment and receivables) versus what it owes (liabilities like supplier dues and loans). The difference is your capital — the owner's stake. Even a rough monthly balance sheet shows whether the business is growing.

Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-lasting asset — a fridge, a vehicle, a counter — over the years you use it, instead of treating it as one big expense. This gives a truer picture of yearly profit and is also allowed as a deduction when calculating income tax.

Reconcile your records with your bank and cash box regularly. Count the cash, compare it with what the books say should be there, and match bank entries to your statement. Catching small mismatches early prevents big confusion at month end and at tax time.
