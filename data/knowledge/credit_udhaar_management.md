# Managing udhaar (credit) and recovering dues

Giving udhaar builds customer loyalty but ties up your cash and carries the risk of non-payment. The aim is to offer credit to reliable customers in controlled amounts, while keeping enough cash flowing to run and restock the shop.

Set a credit limit for each customer based on how well you know them and their payment history. When a customer reaches their limit, politely ask for part payment before giving more. This stops any single unpaid balance from growing so large that it becomes hard to recover.

Agree clear payment terms up front — for example payment within 15 or 30 days. Writing the due date in the khata, and telling the customer at the time of sale, sets a shared expectation and makes a later reminder feel routine rather than confrontational.

Aging of receivables means sorting what is owed by how long it has been outstanding: current, 30 days, 60 days, 90+ days. Older dues are harder to recover, so chase them first. Reviewing aging weekly highlights which customers need a reminder or a firmer follow-up.

Gentle, consistent reminders recover most dues. Send a polite message a few days before the due date, again on the due date, and a friendly follow-up after. Most late payments happen because customers simply forget, so timely reminders collect money without spoiling the relationship.

Offer small incentives for prompt payment, such as a tiny discount for clearing dues within the term, and be firmer with repeat slow payers by reducing their limit. Rewarding good behaviour and limiting risky behaviour gradually improves your whole customer book.

Keep proof of every udhaar transaction — the date, amount, and what it was for — confirmed with the customer. A clear, agreed record prevents disputes later and, if a balance is ever contested, shows exactly what was given and what was paid.

For large or stubborn dues, a written acknowledgement of the debt from the customer, or settling through partial instalments, is better than letting the amount drift. The goal is steady recovery, because money collected today can be reinvested in stock that earns again.
